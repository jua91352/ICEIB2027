% S = Simulation setup
\subsection{Dataset and Setup}
We use historical daily vegetable prices from 13 public markets in Taichung
City~\cite{taichung2026retail}, reported as market averages in NT\$ per catty
(600\,g). The publisher uses a price of zero to mean that no price was
recorded; such entries are treated as missing rather than as a $-100\%$
change.
% VERIFY(author): confirm that the DIC3 pipeline drops zero prices.
Each sample is a (vegetable, market, day
$t$) triple whose target is the relative change $\Delta_{t,h}$ of
\eqref{eq:delta}. The data are split chronologically: models are trained on
the earlier period and tested once on a held-out later period
(\todo{train and test date ranges}). The held-out test sets contain
$n=2{,}085$ samples for $h=7$ and $n=1{,}859$ samples for $h=14$.

To prevent leakage~\cite{kaufman2012leakage}, a training sample at day $t$ is
kept only if its target date $t+h$ falls before the split date; 1{,}386
samples whose labels had not yet matured were removed. All features use
information available on day $t$ only. Models are implemented in Python with
scikit-learn~\cite{pedregosa2011scikit}.

% Q = Quality and quantity (metrics), R = Related methods to compare
\subsection{Baselines and Metrics}
Two simple but strong baselines are used, following the forecasting practice
of always comparing with naive methods~\cite{makridakis2020m4}:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textit{No-change (persistence):} $\hat{\Delta}_{t,h}=0$, i.e., the
price stays as it is.
\item \textit{Momentum:} the most recent relative change continues,
$\hat{\Delta}_{t,h}=(M_t-M_{t-h})/M_{t-h}$.
% VERIFY(author): confirm the momentum window used in DIC3 (h days or 7 days).
\end{itemize}

\textit{Direction macro-F1.} Each change is mapped to a class
$c\in\mathcal{C}=\{\text{down},\text{flat},\text{up}\}$ using a tolerance
$\tau$ around zero (\todo{value of $\tau$}). With $F_1^{(c)}$ the F1-score of
class $c$,
\begin{equation}
\text{macro-F1}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}|}\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}F_1^{(c)} .
\label{eq:f1}
\end{equation}
Macro averaging prevents the dominant ``flat'' class from hiding errors on the
rare but important up and down moves.

\textit{Relative-change MAE.}
\begin{equation}
\text{MAE}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl|\Delta_i-\hat{\Delta}_i\bigr| .
\label{eq:mae}
\end{equation}
Higher macro-F1 and lower MAE are better.

\subsection{Discussion}
\textit{Why are the baselines hard to beat?} In our data, 86.7--92.8\% of
seven-day price pairs have exactly the same price at both ends. Such sticky
series~\cite{nakamura2008five,bils2004some} make ``no change'' close to optimal
for absolute error, while momentum captures the few persistent moves, for
example after a typhoon. This agrees with large forecasting studies in which
simple methods remain competitive~\cite{makridakis2020m4,zeng2023transformers}.

\textit{RQ1 and RQ2.} At both 7 and 14 days, the evidence does not support
using RF/HGB forecasts alone; the momentum signal deserves to be shown to the
user. Whether mode B improves on mode A must be judged from the verified values
in Table~\ref{tab:comparison}.

\textit{RQ3.} The web prototype answers a natural-language query with mode A
and mode B forecasts side by side, and every number shown comes from a tool
call. This shows that low-effort querying is technically feasible; a user
study is still needed to measure usefulness.

\textit{Threats to validity.} The target is the public market price, which is
only a proxy for the price in a specific store; results come from one city and
one period; and the direction metric depends on $\tau$.
